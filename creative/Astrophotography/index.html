Astrophotography

Long-exposure frames from dark skies.

Space was my first special interest, though it started before I can remember. My earliest memory of it is my dad telling me Pluto wasn't a planet anymore, from a top bunk, before I was old enough to go to school. In 2010 he gave me a SkyScout, a handheld device you point at the sky and it tells you what you're looking at. He also built a roll-off roof observatory from his own design when I was a kid, built several more for other people, and eventually gave me his old Dwarf II when he upgraded. I grew up expecting humans to be on Mars by now, watching the Constellation programme get quietly shelved and replaced with decades of delays and modest milestones. I studied biochemistry and genetics and ended up writing computational pipelines for protein engineering, which is not as far from astrophysics as it sounds, because the universe reuses the same mathematics from the quantum to the cosmic scale and that fact has never stopped being interesting to me. The underlying question, the one that connects all of it, is whether there is anyone out there. Nobody knows yet. That feels like the most important open problem there is.

These images were taken with a telescope that weighs as much as a laptop. It sits on a garden wall in Kent, connects to a phone over WiFi, and does its own stacking and tracking automatically while you go inside and make tea. The garden is surrounded on most sides by fruit farms whose polytunnels glow all night and bounce off the cloud cover. Most of these images were taken on nights when the sky was actually clear, which is the only time the light pollution collapses to something manageable. You plan around the weather, you wait, and then you go outside at eleven in the evening and point it at something that has been there since before your species existed.

There is a theory I have, probably original but probably not, that humans have a specific evolved response to finding the Andromeda galaxy beautiful, because it is the only naked-eye galaxy, and for a hundred thousand years it reliably meant clear autumn skies and the abundance window before winter. The response is still firing. The context it evolved in is long gone. I find that enormously comforting for reasons I cannot fully articulate. The science belongs to everyone. These images are not Mars. But the light in them left before I was born, the telescope came from my dad, and that feels like enough of a start.
